# Names and faces recipe

Remembering names is the highest-demand real-world memory task, and the hardest, because a name is
an arbitrary label with no inherent meaning. The fix is to *give it meaning* and *anchor it to a
face*.

## The four steps

1. **Attend.** Most "forgetting" is failure to encode — you never heard the name. Repeat it back
   once at introduction ("Good to meet you, Priya"). This alone fixes much of the problem.

2. **Make the name an image.** Convert the sound of the name into something picturable:
   - Sounds-like substitution: *Priya* → "pre-ya" → a **prayer**; *Baker* → a **baker**;
     *Sandra* → **sand** + a **ra**y of sun.
   - Meaningful names (occupational, nature, place) are easiest — use the literal meaning.

3. **Find a distinctive feature.** Pick the single most salient thing about the face — strong brow,
   dimple, hairline, glasses, jaw. One feature, not a forensic catalogue.

4. **Bind image to feature, vividly.** Stage the name-image interacting with the feature: a tiny
   baker kneading dough on Mr. Baker's prominent chin; sand pouring from Sandra's curls. Bizarre,
   multisensory, exaggerated — the standard `loci-encoder` levers.

## Placing in a palace

For a *room full* of new people (a meeting, a class), place each name-image at a station along the
route in the order you met them — then walk the route to recall the room. For one-off introductions,
the face itself is the locus; no palace needed.

## Review

Names decay fast. Hand the set to `spaced-recall` for a short review the same day and again within a
few days, or you will lose them by the next meeting.
